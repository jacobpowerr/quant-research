# Testing the Hurst Exponent in Financial Markets:
# Null Models and Trend-Continuation Prediction

## 1. Research Objective

The Hurst exponent is often interpreted as a measure of persistence or long-range dependence in a time series. Values above 0.5 are commonly associated with persistent behaviour, while values below 0.5 are associated with mean-reverting behaviour. However, estimated Hurst values can deviate from 0.5 because of finite-sample variation rather than genuine market structure.

This project investigates whether the Hurst exponent provides meaningful information about financial markets. It asks three main questions:

1. Do ETF returns produce Hurst estimates that differ materially from estimates generated by independent Gaussian noise?
2. Does the rolling behaviour of the Hurst exponent exhibit unusual structure relative to an IID null model?
3. Does the rolling Hurst exponent improve out-of-sample predictions of trend continuation beyond momentum and volatility?

The purpose is not to confirm that the Hurst exponent is useful, but to test that claim against explicit null models and unseen data.

## 2. Data

The dataset contains daily closing prices for 20 exchange-traded funds:

DBA, DBC, EEM, EFA, FXE, FXY, GLD, HYG, IEF, IWM, LQD, QQQ, SLV, SPY, TLT, USO, UUP, VNQ, XLE, and XLK.

These ETFs provide exposure to several markets, including equities, bonds, commodities, currencies, and real estate. Using multiple asset classes reduces the risk that the results are driven entirely by the behaviour of one particular market.

The common price sample begins on February 6, 2006.

Daily closing prices were converted into log returns. These returns were the input for every Hurst calculation in the project.

The Hurst exponent was estimated in two forms. First, one full-sample Hurst value was calculated for each ETF. These estimates were compared with Hurst values generated from simulated IID Gaussian returns. Second, a rolling Hurst value was calculated for each ETF using a 252-trading-day window. These rolling estimates were analyzed as an object of interest and later used as an input feature in the logistic regression experiment.

Hurst therefore plays two roles in this project: it is first the statistic being tested and later a potential predictor of trend continuation.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Hurst Estimation

The Hurst exponent was estimated from each ETF’s log-return series using the `compute_Hc` function from Python’s `hurst` package with `kind="change"`.

Two forms of the estimate were calculated:

- **Full-sample Hurst:** One Hurst value was calculated for each ETF using its complete series of 4,463 log returns. This provided a broad measure of persistence across the available sample.
- **Rolling Hurst:** A new Hurst value was calculated each trading day using the previous 252 log returns, approximately one year of market data. This produced a time-varying estimate that could be examined for changes in market behaviour.

Each rolling window ended on the date associated with its estimate. It therefore used only current and historical returns, preventing future information from entering the calculation.

The full-sample estimate was used to test whether each ETF displayed unusual persistence across the complete period. The rolling estimate was used to examine whether persistence changed over time and was later included as a feature in the predictive model.

### 3.2 Full-Sample Null Test

The full-sample null test examined whether the Hurst estimates from the ETFs were unusual relative to estimates produced by a process with no temporal dependence.

The null hypothesis was that each ETF’s Hurst estimate was consistent with an IID Gaussian return process. Under this model, each return is generated independently from the same normal distribution, meaning that past returns provide no information about future returns.

To construct the null distribution, 1,000 simulated return series were generated using NumPy with a fixed random seed. Each simulation contained 4,463 IID Gaussian returns, matching the length of the ETF return series. The Hurst exponent was then calculated for every simulated series using the same method applied to the ETF data.

This process produced an empirical distribution of Hurst estimates under the null hypothesis. Matching the sample length was important because the Hurst estimator can deviate from 0.5 through finite-sample variation, even when the underlying data are completely independent.

Each ETF’s observed Hurst estimate was compared with this null distribution to calculate a p-value representing how unusual the estimate was under IID noise. Because 20 ETFs were tested, a Bonferroni correction was applied. The family-wide significance level of 0.05 was divided by 20, producing a corrected significance threshold of 0.0025.

### 3.3 Rolling-Hurst Null Test

The rolling-Hurst null test examined whether the behaviour of the ETFs’ rolling Hurst estimates differed from what could arise from independent Gaussian returns.

Because calculating thousands of rolling Hurst values was computationally expensive, 100 IID Gaussian return series were simulated. Each series contained 4,463 observations with a mean of 1 and a standard deviation of 1, matching the length used in the ETF analysis. A 252-day rolling Hurst exponent was then calculated across each simulated series using the same procedure applied to the ETFs.

Four properties of each rolling-Hurst series were examined:

- Mean
- Standard deviation
- Minimum
- Maximum

These produced a separate null distribution for each summary statistic.

For each statistic, the observed ETF value was compared with the centre of its simulated null distribution. An empirical two-sided p-value was calculated by measuring the absolute distance between the ETF statistic and the null mean, then finding the proportion of simulated statistics whose distances were at least as large.

A z-score was also calculated by subtracting the null mean from the observed ETF statistic and dividing the result by the standard deviation of the corresponding null distribution. Positive z-scores indicate values above the null mean, while negative z-scores indicate values below it.

Because each statistic was tested across 20 ETFs, a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of 0.0025 was used.

### 3.4 Predictive Experiment

The predictive experiment tested whether rolling Hurst improved predictions of trend continuation beyond information already contained in momentum and volatility.

The target, `Trend_Continuation`, was binary. It equalled 1 when the asset’s current direction matched its direction over the following 21 trading days and 0 when the directions differed.

Future direction was determined by taking the sign of the change in log price between the current date and 21 trading days later. Current direction was determined by taking the sign of the change in log price over the previous 63 trading days.

Two logistic regression models were trained:

- **Baseline model:** Momentum and volatility
- **Hurst model:** Momentum, volatility, and rolling Hurst

The purpose of this comparison was to isolate the incremental value of Hurst. If the Hurst model consistently outperformed the baseline, this would indicate that Hurst contained predictive information beyond the other two features.

The data were divided chronologically using unique trading dates. Approximately 70% of the dates were assigned to training, 15% to validation, and 15% to testing. The rows were not shuffled because doing so would mix past and future market observations.

A gap of 21 trading days was left between the training and validation periods and between the validation and test periods. These gaps prevented targets near the end of one period from using prices belonging to the following period.

The features were standardized using scikit-learn’s `StandardScaler`. For each feature, the scaler subtracted its training-set mean and divided by its training-set standard deviation. The scaler was fitted only on the training data and then applied unchanged to the validation and test data, preventing information from the future periods from entering the training process.

Model performance was evaluated using four comparisons:

- **Naive accuracy:** The accuracy obtained by always predicting the more common outcome.
- **Log loss:** The quality of the predicted probabilities, with larger penalties for confident incorrect predictions.
- **ROC AUC:** The model’s ability to rank trend continuations above reversals across all classification thresholds.
- **Accuracy:** The proportion of correct classifications when probabilities of at least 0.5 were classified as trend continuations.

### 4.1 Full-Sample Null-Test Results

DBC and USO produced the most unusual full-sample Hurst estimates relative to the IID Gaussian null distribution. Both estimates were noticeably above the centre of the simulated distribution and produced unadjusted p-values below 0.05.

However, 20 ETFs were tested simultaneously. After applying the Bonferroni correction, neither result met the corrected significance threshold of 0.0025. No other ETF produced a statistically significant result.

The null hypothesis was therefore not rejected for any of the 20 ETFs. Under this methodology, the observed full-sample Hurst estimates were consistent with the range of estimates that could arise from finite samples of IID Gaussian returns. The analysis did not provide sufficient evidence of persistence beyond what could be produced by random independent data.
